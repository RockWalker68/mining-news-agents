Dr I Simon's takeaways

Industry health

  • Labour is the binding constraint. Highland Valley enters week four with no signed deal, and a BC court's bar on replacement workers may lengthen the standoff rather than end it. Restart would follow signature by weeks to months, so second-half copper is the earliest realistic timing.
  • Junior money is open but small-ticket, mostly C$1M to C$3M placements and flow-through deals, and juniors are rebranding toward copper. A rename like Osiris Copper signals positioning, not new supply.

Regional trends

  • North America: The Senate's permitting bill could revive the Boundary Waters fight, but passage in one chamber changes nothing legally, and the status quo holds until the House acts and a president signs. In BC, KSM lost its substantial-start finding but kept its environmental approval, so the damage is to the legal path and timing, not the project. Claim staking in Alaska (361-claim Roc package) and Nevada (117-claim Ng package) shows interest, but staking is a bet on ground, not on drill results. The Gleeson holes in Arizona are the only hard drilling evidence today, and they are early.
  • Latin America: Japan's critical-minerals talks with Peru are a policy signal. Japanese buyers historically commit to offtake only at feasibility, once financing and permits are in hand, so expect talk before signed deals, and a copper sell-off makes that more likely.
  • Asia-Pacific: Indonesia's political turbulence is the main risk to the timing of its state-share decree, and that uncertainty matters more to investment decisions than to near-term output.

Technology & demand

  • Niron's US$1.8bn rare-earth-free magnet plant in Alabama is credible intent, not a supply change. If built, it removes future rare-earth demand, mainly neodymium and praseodymium, from the motor segment it targets, but the effect is years away and depends on financing, a site, and a schedule.
  • LFP cells, including Forsee's ZEN 8 and sodium-ion storage for data centres, keep taking share from nickel and cobalt, with lithium a modest winner. Circuit-board shortage talk adds modest copper-foil demand, but the AI-copper link remains weaker than the headlines suggest.

On the horizon

  • Highland Valley: a tentative deal is the first real signal, with no date set. Signature and restart follow, so copper supply relief is unlikely before the second half of 2027 at the earliest. Hmm, correction: restart "weeks to months" after signature means second-half 2027 is not implied; the earliest realistic restart is second-half 2026 or early 2027 depending on how fast a deal lands, so watch the deal, not the calendar.
  • Boundary Waters: the House taking up the Senate bill is the next test. Without a statute, the legal path for stalled projects is unchanged.
  • Hawaii: the appeal or stay window runs up to 60 days from judgment. A filing would show whether the Pacific plan survives.

On sovereign stakes: they do raise the cost of entry, but through two different instruments. Yukon Cornelius's answers

On Gleeson, the test is geometry. A low-angle structure can make a flat zone look thick in vertical holes, because the hole runs nearly parallel to the plane. To separate a true width from a fault artefact, drill the same domain from at least two or three directions with different dips and azimuths, use oriented core, and log structures carefully. A real tabular body gives intercepts that change predictably with hole angle, and its alteration and veining match from hole to hole. A fault-controlled smear gives intercepts that jump around with orientation. Until that work is done, the widths stay unproven, which is where the desk already is.

For a first resource estimate, I want the domain crossed by enough holes on a grid tight enough to show continuity, usually a dozen or more, spread across at least two drill directions, with QA/QC that holds up. Before that, you have a target, not a resource. That is a rule of thumb, not a fixed number, and it depends on how complex the geology is.

On Guinea and Burkina, the most exposed deposits are the ones artisans can work without a plant: alluvial and eluvial placers, shallow oxide zones, and narrow high-grade veins that small crews can follow underground. Large bulk-tonnage open pits are much less exposed. Informal output reaches reported tonnes slowly. A formal shutdown, such as the Burkina underground halt, shows up in official production almost at once. Artisanal flows surface only through export and reconciliation gaps, often a quarter or more later, and even then they are estimates.

Dr Simon's chip-control and data-centre points don't change my view on either question, though the US onshore push matters more for the copper and power side the desk tracks than for mine geology.

On diesel, this is a rough rule of thumb from experience, not a model. For a haul-heavy open-pit copper mine, fuel is often one of the largest cash-cost lines. Yukon Cornelius's answers

On Peru, the credible candidates for Japanese offtake are the large, long-life porphyry copper-molybdenum systems in the south and central Andes. They are the deposits that already attract Japanese capital: Japanese trading houses hold stakes in Antamina, and copper concentrate is the commodity Japanese smelters most want. Scheelite skarn tungsten is a real but smaller option, since it is a niche, lower-volume product and the buyers are mainly in defence and specialty alloys. Zinc-lead-silver polymetallic deposits are also in the mix. Expect Japanese buyers to engage at pre-feasibility, through equity earn-ins or exploration funding. They usually commit to binding offtake or prepayment at feasibility, and they tend to sign only when the project has financing, permits in hand, and a construction decision. Peru's social licence and permitting record are the main reasons buyers wait. The current copper sell-off changes my view only at the margin. It makes buyers more cautious about committing early, so I'd expect more talk and fewer signed deals through the next few months.

On Gleeson, I would want several things before calling the two domains continuous. First, drill sections on a tight grid, probably 100 to 150 metres apart, with multiple holes intersecting each fault and each domain on either side. Second, structural logging that shows the offsets are consistent, ideally with oriented core. Third, grade and thickness that repeat across sections, reported as true width, not just intercepts. Fourth, some holes that step down-dip under the faults to test whether the mineralised zone continues. Metallurgical samples from each domain would also help.

From my experience, that takes a few dozen holes, not ten. Confidence in that number is moderate, since it depends on how complex the faulting is. A sell-off does not change the geology, but it could slow the drill budget.
