Meldrum's answers

Short answer on all three: the material doesn't yet let me separate timing from margin, so my reads should move with the disclosures.

On the war premium versus a real cost shock: a premium unwinding shows up first in share prices and market value, which is where the $712 billion figure sits, and it doesn't touch costs. A real energy or freight shock should appear in unit-cost lines (diesel, power, reagents, freight) and in revised cost guidance. The earliest filing is the next quarterly production and cost report, or any guidance update that restates costs. I should also flag that the war framing is unsourced in our notes, so I can't yet tie either effect to it.

On Chile, Dr Simon is right that my inference rests on premises we haven't verified. I assumed volume fell, but no tonnes are published. If tonnes are confirmed down, I'd treat a margin story as more likely while the price forecast keeps rising. I'd move to a demand story if the price forecast is cut alongside lower volume, if LME or exchange stocks rise, or if Chinese refined copper import data weakens. Cochilco's cost data would test the margin reading directly, so I'd weight that above any forecast revision.

On Endeavour, I'll concede that my lean toward margin has outrun the evidence. I'd reclassify the halt as a margin problem only if cost or production guidance is cut, or if the restart runs beyond roughly one quarter with a capital requirement attached. Publishing a restart date changes the test: the date itself is then the benchmark, and a second slip would be the stronger signal of a development or ground-control problem, which is closer to Yukon Cornelius's reading. I agree the restart-date prediction should carry the weight, and I'd rather be tested on timing than argue cause from a halt notice alone.

Yukon Cornelius's answers

Endeavour. The disclosure I'd accept first is a ramp-advance figure: metres advanced per month, compared with plan and with earlier periods. It's measured, dated, and hard to spin, and it shows whether the drive has stalled or is just slow. A geotechnical notice naming ground support, water inflow, or a structure would be the strongest evidence of a ground-control problem. A management statement of schedule is the weakest on its own, because it's a promise rather than a measurement. I should also be clear that my development-problem read is still a hypothesis. Sinking ramps stall for many reasons (poor ground, water, faulting, equipment), and nothing we have shows which one applies. The restart-date prediction is the cleaner test, so the cause can stay open until it resolves.

Nyam. The desk should log the medium version, without true width. True width needs a structural model of the orebody, the dip of the zone, and the orientation of the hole. An unnamed promotional source is unlikely to supply that in a release, and grade and drilled interval are what a release normally gives. Scoring should wait until the company is named, because the prediction refers to "the company behind" a headline and can't be checked against an anonymous source. Once it's named, the test is simple. My confidence on the numbers is medium if a real release exists. The bigger risk is whether anyone publishes one.

Northern Star. Before reading the output rise as more than timing, I'd want the same quarter last year and the prior quarter in ounces, the company's annual guidance range, and all-in sustaining cost (AISC) for the period against that guidance. I'd also want head grade, tonnes milled, and recovery, to see whether the rise came from higher grade, higher throughput, or drawing down stockpiles. If output rose and AISC held, that's either real productivity or pulled-forward ore. If output rose and AISC climbed, the gain was bought at a cost. Until those lines are in front of us, the Nikkei headline is a number sitting next to a takeover story.
