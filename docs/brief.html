Dr I Simon's takeaways

Industry health

  • Labour and cost are the binding constraints. Highland Valley is in its fourth week with scab allegations, which points to a hardening dispute, and Gulf shipping risk reaches North American diesel, freight and sulphur costs first.
  • Disruption now also comes from health and politics. Barrick's Congo furlough over Ebola concerns hits Kibali, a gold mine rather than a copper-cobalt operation, but it shows disease can idle output as quickly as a strike. Mongolia's president's resignation over alleged corruption adds policy risk to Oyu Tolgoi, which has already lost about 70% of its exports to Chinese border checks.

Regional trends

  • North America: Federal money is flowing to tungsten and antimony, through Ottawa's up to C$14m for Yukon tungsten, Fireweed's Mactung and Nova Minerals' final Alaska antimony permits. I read part of this as defence demand, which makes it more durable than the metal's size suggests, while Highland Valley remains the main supply risk.
  • Latin America: Cobre Panama talks have begun, but a restart is years away and gated by water treatment, a plant reopening decision and state terms. Chile's private-role talk remains intent until a named Codelco asset appears.
  • Africa: Guinea's permits to PDI Gold are a 2028 story for formal tonnes, while the near-term squeeze falls on artisanal gold that is being stockpiled behind export rules. The expulsion, jailing and displacement figures are unconfirmed, so treat them as reported headlines until a named source backs them.

Technology & demand

  • LFP grid storage, including Samsung SDI's deal, keeps taking share from nickel and cobalt, while recycling growth (Veolia's French site, India's black mass export ban) adds secondary nickel, cobalt and lithium supply at home.
  • Sodium-ion (Draslovka's Natron IP) and nickel-free cathode processes (Umicore) are early and would trim long-run lithium and nickel demand, but their timing is uncertain. Tungsten and rare earths gain from defence and Quad policy, with China's licences the swing factor.

On the horizon

  • Highland Valley settlement: a deal would start a restart, but output returns over weeks to months after signing, so the copper effect lags.
  • Trump's Asia trip: the key test is whether China publishes multi-year export licence terms for antimony, tungsten and rare earths. Watch the spread between Chinese domestic prices and Rotterdam or New York prices.
  • Cobre Panama and Indonesia: watch for a dated restart timetable at Cobre Panama and a decree with numeric state-share or royalty terms in Indonesia, which would turn intent into policy.

Meldrum & Yukon Cornelius: short takes

Meldrum Strategist

Labour remains copper's binding constraint, with Highland Valley's strike entering week four and Barrick's Congo furlough showing that health shutdowns can idle African output as fast as a walkout. Chinese concentrate smelting is near its ceiling, and Hormuz diesel risk would hit North American costs first. Critical-metals policy is the bright spot, though much of it is still intent rather than tonnes.

Yukon Cornelius Geologist

Copper's near-term supply is being decided by keeping existing mines running, not by new projects. Highland Valley's strike and Barrick's Congo furlough show how quickly labour and health events can idle output. Permits and tungsten and antimony progress are real, but they are years away from tonnes.

Dr I Simon's creative angles
  • If US chip curbs move data-centre and grid build to the US, India and the Gulf, copper demand relocates toward North America, but transformer lead times and permitting, not metal supply, become the bottleneck. (speculation level: medium)
  • Guinea's stockpiled gold could release quickly if export rules ease, so the 2028 framing for formal supply could be wrong in the other direction, with a near-term supply rush rather than a squeeze. (speculation level: high)
  • An Oyu Tolgoi export pause hits contractor payments before tonnes, so the first casualties may be the smaller service firms building the underground ramp rather than the mine itself. (speculation level: high)
Dr I Simon's global view

Trump's Asia trip is the widest-reaching event. Reported deals in Manila and Shenzhen, a summit involving Xi and Putin, and a possible Kim meeting will shape the terms on which Chinese rare-earth, antimony and tungsten licences are issued, as Meldrum noted. Meanwhile the US is moving to curb China's remote access to AI chips, and a China data-centre curb is advancing despite lobbying. For mining, these curbs slow the AI build in China and may shift it elsewhere. They don't remove the copper, power and grid demand behind it, so I read them as relocating demand, not cancelling it. Japan and the Philippines pledging to strengthen economic security points the same way: Asian mineral supply is being tied to security blocs.

Mongolia's president announcing his resignation over alleged corruption matters for copper. Oyu Tolgoi has already lost 70% of its exports to Chinese border checks, and a change of government adds policy risk to a mine that depends on China. Ukraine's strikes on the Crimea bridge and Russian strikes on Kyiv's bridges are logistics stories for Europe's energy and metals trade, though the record shows no direct metals effect. A reported LPG tanker sinking off Bulgaria and the Iran dirty-bomb report add to Black Sea and Gulf shipping risk, which feeds diesel and freight costs for North American mines. Confidence in the cost effect is medium; the policy effects are low.

Yukon Cornelius's answers

On the Congo restart question: First, a correction to the framing. Barrick's Congolese asset is Kibali, a gold mine, not a copper-cobalt operation. The copper-cobalt restart question belongs to the Katanga operators such as Kamoa-Kakula, Mutanda, and Tenke Fungurume.

Restart time depends on how each mine was idled:

  • Open-pit and heap-leach operations restart in weeks, because the work is mainly crews, power, and reagents.
  • Underground operations take longer, because water rises in idle workings and has to be pumped out, ventilation has to be restored, and ground support has to be inspected before anyone goes back underground. Shafts and ramps near water-bearing ground are the slowest. In my experience, a short furlough at an underground mine costs weeks, but a long one with flooding can cost months.
  • Ebola adds a people constraint. Medical clearance and rehiring can hold up a restart even when the ground is fine.

So the mines that take longest are the deep, wet, underground ones that have been left unpumped. I'd treat a furlough of a few weeks as a short-term output cut, not a structural one.

On Oyu Tolgoi: This changes my view. I had treated the underground ramp as an engineering story, and the desk has not connected it to Mongolia's politics. The ramp is largely built by contractors and funded by mine cash flow. A pause in China-facing exports hits cash first, and a cash squeeze then slows contractor payments and capital calls, which slows development. Physical output moves later. Concentrate stockpiles and mill throughput can carry the operation for weeks to months, so sales fall before tonnes do. My confidence that a political shock slows the underground schedule is medium. The timing of any output effect is low confidence, because it depends on how much stockpile the mine holds.
