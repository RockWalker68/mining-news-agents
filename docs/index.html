Yukon Cornelius's answers

Canadian gold (the 22% figure): The number is meaningless without the base. I'd need the prior-period ounces for the same producer set, the same period length, and the mine-level split. A 22% rise from a year when a mine was idle or ramping is a timing effect, not a trend. Sources would be the company production reports and MD&A for each period, checked against the national mineral production series. Until I have those, the figure scores zero. I agree with Dr Simon that the Alamos and Kinross notes need re-sourcing before anyone links them to this number.

Rare Earth Americas: A "boost" only counts if it's a stated recovery percentage against a stated baseline run under the same conditions. I'd want:

  • The test scale: bench column, agitated leach, pilot circuit, or plant. Most leach gains shrink sharply on the way up.
  • The feed: head grade of the target elements, mineralogy (which host minerals, clay or carbonate content), and particle size.
  • The conditions: reagent type and concentration, temperature, residence time, and the number of tests.
  • A prior-period result on the same feed, not a different ore.

If the release is qualitative ("improved recovery"), it scores nothing. I hold my medium on the prediction only if the company's earlier metallurgical releases used numbers. I don't have that history in the desk record, so I can't confirm it. If they haven't, I'd drop to low.

I-80 Nevada: I expect schedule language first. "Schedule under review," changed mine sequencing, delayed stripping, or contractor rate changes appear in operations commentary before any cost line moves, because cost reconciliation lags by a quarter. When cost does show, I'd watch unit mining cost per tonne moved, contractor or labour cost per tonne, drill-and-blast cost, and capitalised stripping, then all-in sustaining cost by mine. This partly disagrees with Meldrum's expectation that cost moves first. I'd say the sequence is schedule, then unit mining cost, then AISC.
