Dr I Simon's takeaways

Industry health

  • Labour and permits, not prices, now set the pace of new supply. Highland Valley is in its fourth week with a replacement-worker order in place, and a BC court has reversed a consultation shortcut, so new tonnes are arriving later than the desk assumed.
  • Gold's slide below US$3,800 squeezes juniors first while majors such as Agnico keep spending above US$3.5bn. Chinese premiums and Codelco's 2027 premium cut both look like sales pushes, not shortages.

Regional trends

  • North America: Canada's federal money is going to tungsten (up to C$14M for Mactung, plus funding for the Yukon project), a defence-linked policy bet that means years before tonnes. US seabed leasing is now clouded by the Hawaii ruling, and a restored Boundary Waters moratorium is a proposal whose odds the midterm result may have raised.
  • Africa: Kenya's award of a large critical-minerals contract to a US consortium and Zimbabwe's lithium export-tax change with a local-sourcing mandate both show resource nationalism rising as critical minerals gain strategic value.
  • Latin America: Codelco's dust-permit threat in Chile is a reminder that operating permits are live risks, and El Niño water and power stress in Peru would likely show up first as higher costs before any output loss.

Technology & demand

  • Ford's Michigan LFP cells, built on CATL technology, take demand from nickel and cobalt and add to lithium, iron and phosphate. The shift is now production, but North American battery supply depends on a Chinese licensor.
  • Electrification, which the IEA says the Hormuz crisis has accelerated, adds copper and lithium demand over time, while sodium-ion and solid-state efforts trim long-run lithium and nickel demand but remain early. Transformer shortages are the nearer copper-related bottleneck, and data centres still reach copper only through grids.

On the horizon

  • A signed Highland Valley deal is the swing event. Restart would follow over weeks to months, so a signature alone would not add 2026 tonnes.
  • The Hawaii appeal and the US deep-sea lease sale. A government appeal can be filed up to 60 days after judgment, so a filing may come after mid-December and would still be ordinary.
  • Trump says the US will "see what happens" with Iran after January 1. Hormuz disruption would raise diesel and sulphur costs for North American and Chilean operations before it touched metal output.

Dr I Simon's review

Global view

The most consequential world event for mining this week is APEC in Shenzhen, where Trump is meeting Xi and, reportedly, Putin in a three-way format, with trade on the agenda. No headline mentions critical minerals. The desk's central test, whether China publishes multi-year licence terms for antimony, tungsten and rare earths, sits inside this meeting, so the link is my inference, not reporting. I expect any outcome to be framed as trade stability, with minerals detail arriving later, if at all.

The war in Ukraine keeps munitions demand high. Reports of Russia's low-cost munitions and fresh strikes on the Crimean Bridge point to sustained consumption, which supports the defence-linked tungsten and antimony money that Ottawa and Washington are now committing. This is the one part of the critical-minerals pull that does not depend on a battery cycle.

Japan's carmakers agreeing to standardise key parts and materials could narrow the battery chemistries automakers buy, which would shift the mix of nickel, cobalt and lithium. The effect is early and uncertain.

Anthropic's IPO and the Nvidia headlines feed the AI-buildout narrative that has moved copper's price. That is market sentiment, not grid demand, and I would keep it out of copper tonnage forecasts.

Myanmar's genocide verdict at the UN court has no direct mining content. Kachin state is a source of heavy rare earths, so sanctions and supply-chain scrutiny could follow over a longer horizon. This is speculative.

Desk review

Meldrum's frame that labour, not ore, is the binding constraint is the most solid call on the desk. It holds across Highland Valley, the Barrick Congo furlough and Chile's Q3 contraction. Yukon's point that money and legal action are moving faster than tonnes is the sharpest synthesis, and Meldrum's version of it, that development finance is running ahead of tonnes, is the same insight. Both are right to keep the Codelco low provisional.

Some single-source items need more caution than the desk gave them. Mexico's below-median AISC is one district's data point, as Yukon rightly argued. The Manyar premium, the 100,000-person Mali figure and the Nunavut closure warning each rest on one report, so they should stay as context. Meldrum's "formal supply possibly rising" in Guinea is a 2028 story at the earliest, as Yukon says.

The global view changes the timing of the desk's China licence prediction. APEC puts the Trump–Xi talks in the same week as the test, so the event is closer than "no date set" suggests. Even so, a trade framework without licence terms would be a common outcome, so I hold that prediction at low confidence.

Questions for the strategist

  • Smelters are chasing concentrate, and Freeport Indonesia is pre-selling 2027 copper at a premium. If your concentrate-squeeze reading is right, what should treatment and refining charges do over the next quarter, and what level would tell you the squeeze has ended?
  • Defence money is backing tungsten and antimony while the Ukraine war continues. If a ceasefire came, how much of that funding would survive on civil demand alone, and which end uses would carry it?

Questions for the geologist

  • Ottawa's money for Mactung is tied to defence-driven tungsten demand. From your knowledge of skarn-hosted tungsten, what grade and continuity would Fireweed need to prove before a mine plan could be justified, and how long has that taken in comparable projects?
  • If Chile's low is aging pits and falling grades, what would a Codelco mine plan show over the next two years, in terms of grade drop and tonnes, and which asset would you expect to show the decline first?

Meldrum's answers

On the concentrate squeeze, the cleanest signal is treatment and refining charges. Smelters are paid to take concentrate, so when concentrate is scarce, TCs fall, and when spot TCs go negative, smelters are paying for feed. Over the next quarter I expect spot TCs to stay under pressure while pre-sold tonnes such as Freeport Indonesia's 2027 volumes and the district-level shortfalls keep concentrate tight. I'd call the squeeze over when spot TCs turn clearly positive and keep rising over several weeks, and when the next annual benchmark is set above the current one. One quarterly move proves little; the trend matters. The Manyar premium rests on a single report, so I treat it as context that fits the pattern. Confidence medium.

On defence money, a ceasefire would not remove it all at once. Stockpiling and supply-security logic usually outlasts the fighting, because governments rebuild reserves. Munitions-linked demand is the part that would fall sharply: tungsten for penetrators and antimony for hardening lead in shell and ammunition alloys. Civil demand would carry much more. Tungsten carbide for cutting and mining tools is most of the metal's market. Antimony still has solar glass and some flame-retardant use, though the latter is shrinking. Rare earths would hold on EV and wind magnets. My expectation is that projects with civil offtake survive a ceasefire, while those justified only by munitions procurement get re-tested. As Dr Simon notes, China's licensing decisions probably matter more to these metals' prices than the battlefield does. Confidence medium-low, since no ceasefire is in the headlines and the question is hypothetical.

Yukon Cornelius's answers

On Mactung, skarn-hosted tungsten is a known deposit type. The scheelite forms where hot fluids reacted with limestone near intrusions. Grades are usually modest, so the mine case rests on tonnage, continuity and recovery. To justify a mine plan, Fireweed would need a resource that converts to indicated category over enough strike and depth to support a decade-plus life. It would also need a metallurgical flowsheet that reliably recovers fine-grained scheelite, and geotechnical data for a large open pit in cold northern terrain. Comparable projects have usually taken 10 to 15 years from discovery to production once permitting is included. Mactung's problem has rarely been finding the rock. It is proving the economics and getting through review. Ottawa's money helps with metallurgy and permitting data, not with the ore. The defence link changes who pays for the work, not what the rock is.

On Chile, if aging pits are the cause, a Codelco mine plan should show it in three places: head grade falling year on year at the large open pits, a rising stripping ratio as pits deepen, and flat mill throughput, so copper output falls with grade. Over two years I would expect a sustained grade decline of a few percent a year at mature pits, not a cliff. That is my field judgement, not a sourced figure. The first assets to show it would be the older open-pit complexes feeding low-grade stockpiles. El Teniente is an underground block cave with its own separate issues. The caveat matters: if the 42,100-tonne overstatement is a reserve or grade-model revision rather than a production shortfall, the mine plan will not show it directly. The answer sits in the reconciliation between the model and what the mill actually processed. That is why I keep the Codelco read provisional.

Dr I Simon's conclusions

Industry health

  • Labour and safety, not geology, are setting the pace. The Highland Valley dispute, Harmony's fatalities and Barrick's Congo furlough all idle output, and Chile's 1.1% Q3 contraction is the macro echo of those problems.
  • Copper's price fall is a sentiment shock sitting on a concentrate squeeze. The squeeze will show first in treatment charges, not in this year's tonnes. Gold at year-plus lows will cut marginal exploration budgets over the winter.

Regional trends

  • North America: Canada is building a tungsten policy track through Ottawa's offer of up to C$14M for Mactung and the Yukon funding, but these are development dollars, not tonnes. Hawaii's ruling turns federal seabed leasing into a legal test, and US rare-earth rules tighten in the new year.
  • Latin America: Chile's output story is unresolved. Aging pits and falling grades is the favoured reading, but the 42,100-tonne overstatement could be a data problem. Colombia's AngloGold and Collective copper commitments are the region's largest new copper money, and they are years from production.
  • Africa: Guinea's gold crackdown is pushing artisanal supply out of the formal chain, and the Mali displacement figure remains unconfirmed. Formal permits such as PDI Gold are a 2028 story at the earliest.

Technology & demand

  • Nickel and cobalt keep losing share to LFP. Ford's CATL-licensed Michigan cells for the 2028 Fathom EV are the clearest North American sign. Lithium gains only modestly because LFP still uses it, and iron phosphate is a small tonnage story.
  • Copper and electrical steel gain through transformer and switchgear shortages, which matter more to the AI buildout than the copper price does. Tungsten and antimony gain from munitions-linked money, and rare earths gain modestly from Aclara's first magnet-grade NdPr.

On the horizon
