Dr I Simon's takeaways

Industry health

  • Labour and cost pressure are tightening together. Highland Valley is in its fourth week with no signed deal, and a second BC strike has added to the labour picture. Rising costs and slipping schedules, such as Vizsla's one-year delay at Panuco, mean budgets will tighten before supply does.
  • Capital is still reaching juniors, but prices and freight are weakening the margin base. Copper below US$13,000 and gold below US$3,600 are each reported in one set of notes, so treat them as single-source until wider reporting confirms them. Higher diesel and sulphur costs, from the Houthi and Yemen port risk, reach Chilean and Indonesian supply through price first.

Regional trends

  • North America: The American Samoa lease sale went ahead with about US$6M in bids, reportedly from one bidder, so the desk's call that it would be postponed or halted was wrong. The live question is whether Hawaii's appeal or a stay reaches the leases, and a thin single-bidder result makes the commercial appetite for seabed nodules look weak. Ivanhoe Electric's first Gleeson holes are the clearest real discovery signal in the log, but low-angle geometry means true width and continuity are unproven.
  • Latin America: Chile's problem is state credibility more than supply. The Gates visit has made the question of who should own Chilean copper a public argument, Q3 output contracted 1.1%, and the unexplained Codelco overstatement remains the central governance issue. Junior drilling and financing continue, but they do not change near-term tonnes.
  • Asia-Pacific: Indonesia's policy uncertainty is now a cost-pressure story, with no decree yet, and fire and drought losses add a physical risk the desk had not logged. Japan's February start for a full-scale rare-earth seabed test is a policy milestone that also adds to Western rare-earth intent without adding tonnes.

Technology & demand

  • Copper and electrical steel gain from grid build-out. Grid spending runs through regulated utility plans, so it is steadier than data-centre demand, though a transformer shortage will delay it.
  • Nickel, cobalt and lithium lose share to LFP cells, which Ford has now started producing in Michigan. Sodium-ion storage is early, but it is the first real stationary-storage threat to lithium in this log. Rare earths gain near term, while Niron's rare-earth-free magnets are a long-run counterweight.

On the horizon

  • Highland Valley: a tentative deal is the next swing signal, and whether the second BC strike settles alongside it matters. Even with a deal, restart takes weeks to months, so the second half is the earliest case for meaningful tonnes.
  • Hawaii and American Samoa: watch for an appeal or stay motion against the seabed ruling. A stay that actually halts leases would matter more than the sale itself, and the single-bidder result is the reason to watch it closely.
  • Côte d'Ivoire: royalty changes ahead of IMF talks. Miners are already protesting, and the outcome will show whether West African royalty rises stick or get negotiated down.

Meldrum's answers

Diesel and freight: which operations defer capital first?

Open-pit haul fleets feel it first. Trucks and shovels are among the largest fuel draws on a mine's cost sheet, so a quarter of higher diesel hits operating cost directly. But operations rarely cut operating spending. They defer capital. The first things to slip are fleet replacement, pit expansions with thin margins, and junior development budgets, which depend on financing that gets harder when costs rise.

Heap leaches are less diesel-dependent, but reagent and acid costs, which are tied to sulphur, pass through quickly. Underground operations on grid power are the most insulated, though they still burn diesel in mobile equipment and face power-price exposure.

My ranking: open-pit capital first, heap-leach margins second, electrified underground last. Confidence is medium. A single quarter is a short shock, and most established operators will absorb it.

If AI capex slows, how much of the grid copper case survives?

Most of it, though more slowly and with more risk at the edges. Grid spending runs through regulated rate bases, state and federal programmes, and multi-year utility plans. That is more durable than hyperscaler budgets, which can be cut within a quarter.

The link is not clean, though. Part of the near-term urgency comes from data-centre interconnection requests, and some of those queues would shrink. Transformer and skilled-labour bottlenecks persist regardless.

This changes my earlier view. I had ranked grid build-out close behind China. I now think it is the steadier anchor of the demand case, with the data-centre slice as the cyclical part.

Royalty rises before IMF talks: do they stick?

Historically, the headline rate usually sticks on paper, but the effective take is often negotiated down. Governments need revenue and the IMF generally wants it, so the increase rarely disappears. What gets bargained is the terms: stability agreements, exemptions for existing mines, state equity stakes, or shifting the burden to corporate tax or export levies.

Companies with leverage (long-lived, large, or hard to replace) usually get softer outcomes. Juniors feel it first and often stop spending. Confidence is low to medium on any specific country, because the outcome depends on who is at the table.

Yukon Cornelius's answers

Fire, smoke and drought would reach Indonesian HPAL and nickel operations in different ways and at different speeds. Power is the most direct risk. HPAL plants run on large, steady power loads, often from captive coal plants, and they use sulphuric acid, so a grid or fuel disruption can stop autoclaves and cut throughput quickly. Smoke can also reduce plant efficiency and put pressure on the power supply.

Mine-side effects would come next. Smoke and poor visibility slow haulage and can close pits or roads for a time. Drought affects water for ore processing, slurry handling and tailings, and water is often the constraint that surprises people. Concentrate or ore feed is less of a direct issue for HPAL, since these plants process laterite ore rather than concentrate. The feed problem is more about ore stockpiles running down if mining slows for several weeks.

In production data, the first signal would probably be lower plant utilisation and irregular throughput, reflecting power trips or reduced operating hours. Ore stockpile drawdown and reduced mining rates would follow. Nickel output would show the effect later, and mixed with other factors, so the cause would be hard to isolate. I would treat the fire losses as an unquantified risk for now, not a number to model.

On Dr Simon's point about freight and sulphur, I agree the first pressure is on margins. Higher sulphur and acid costs would squeeze HPAL economics before tonnes fall, and that matters more than the fire question for the next few quarters.

On Gleeson, I would not accept continuity under low-angle faults on the strength of a first set of holes.
